Design requirements (brief recap from G1)
Design Requirement 1: Filtering for Weather & Activity 
Requirement: The system must filter and prioritize clothing options that satisfy the user’s current weather conditions and scheduled activities for the day.
Success Metric: This requirement is met if the user can view a selection of clothes already filtered for the day’s weather and events without having to manually check and exclude incompatible items first.
Design Requirement 2: Visual Outfit Preview
Requirement: The system must provide a way for users to visually assemble and preview how multiple clothing items look together as a single outfit before they are physically worn.
Success Metric: This requirement is met if a user can visually verify that multiple pieces (e.g., top, bottom, and shoes) are well coordinated before they physically put any of the items on.
Design Requirement 3: Availability/Status Tracking
Requirement: The system must track and display the current availability and cleanliness of clothing items within the user's inventory.
Success Metric: This requirement is met if the user can identify which items are currently “unavailable” (e.g., in the laundry) before selecting them for an outfit.
Design Requirement 4: Saved Outfit Archive
Requirement: The system must allow users to save and quickly retrieve combinations of clothing that have been previously verified as successful.
Success Metric: This requirement is met if the user can save a coordinated outfit to a personal library and successfully retrieve it for use at a later date.
Design Requirement 5: Social History Log
Requirement: The system must record and display the date, social context, and audience associated with previously worn outfits.
Success Metric: This requirement is met if the user can view the specific date and the social group or event associated with a previously worn outfit.


These five requirements collectively define both the logistical and social constraints of getting dressed. In the next section, we explore three distinct design directions that each prioritize different subsets of these requirements, allowing us to reason explicitly about tradeoffs before converging on a single direction.
Design alternatives and comparison
Design 1: High-Fidelity Virtual Avatar

Description: This approach uses a personalized 3D avatar that accurately represents the user’s physical proportions and silhouette. Users can “try on” digital versions of their clothing in a virtual environment to see how items work together before physically dressing.
Advantages:
Directly Bridges the Visualization Gap: Makes coordination concrete for users who struggle with mental imagery, addressing the “Spatial Cognition Gap” identified in our findings.
Reduces Physical Friction: Minimizes the need for multiple physical outfit changes, saving time and physical effort during the Trial Phase.
Disadvantages:
Implementation Complexity: Accurate 3D modeling of fabric drape and fit poses a high feasibility barrier compared to 2D alternatives.
Learning Curve: Users may find navigating a 3D interface more time-consuming than traditional mirror-based trials during a rushed morning.
Requirements Traceability:
Supports Well: Design Requirement 2 (Visual Outfit Preview).
Struggles to Support: Design Requirement 1 (Weather/Activity Filtering) and Design Requirement 3 (Laundry/Availability Tracking).
Design 2: Visual Collage & Timestamped Archive

Description: This approach focuses on a 2D “mood board” approach, allowing users to assemble flat-lay collages and save them to a timestamped library. It leverages existing mental models of social media apps (like Instagram) to track when and where outfits were worn.
Advantages:
Low Barrier to Entry: Fits seamlessly into existing user behaviors of taking photos or saving inspiration, making it intuitive to use from day one.
Social & Recency Verification: The timestamped archive allows users to verify social context, solving “Audience Anxiety” by checking exactly when a group last saw an outfit.
Disadvantages:
Manual Upkeep: The system's value depends entirely on the user’s discipline to save and tag outfits; an empty archive provides no utility in a “panic” morning.
Static Representation: Unlike an avatar, a 2D collage cannot show how an outfit fits the body or how layers interact spatially.
Requirements Traceability:
Supports Well: Design Requirement 2 (Visual Outfit Preview), Design Requirement 4 (Saved Outfit Archive), Design Requirement 5 (Social History Log)
Struggles to Support: Design Requirement 1 (Weather and Activity Filtering) and Design Requirement 3 (Laundry and Availability Tracking)
Design 3: Context-Aware Inventory Manager

Description: This approach functions as a digital “control center” for the closet, focusing on item status and logistical constraints. It automatically filters the wardrobe based on real-time weather and scheduled events, while tracking which items are currently clean, dirty, or missing.
Advantages:
Eliminates "Failed" Selections: Prevents the frustration of choosing an outfit only to find a piece is in the laundry or incompatible with the rain.
Reduced Cognitive Load: Automatically handles the “mental sorting” and “survival logistics” of weather/activity, allowing users to jump straight to styling.
Disadvantages:
High Data Input: Requires users to maintain an accurate digital inventory and sync their calendars to provide meaningful event-based filters.
Logistical Rigidity: May struggle with nuanced weather (e.g., "it's sunny but windy") or unexpected changes in daily plans that weren't logged in a calendar.
Requirements Traceability:
Supports Well: Design Requirement 1 (Weather and Activity Filtering) and Design Requirement 3 (Laundry and Availability Tracking).
Struggles to Support: Design Requirement 4 (Saved Outfit Archive) as it focuses on individual items rather than coordinated looks.

Convergence rationale
After evaluating our three alternatives, we have chosen to pursue Design 2: Visual Collage & Timestamped Archive as our primary design direction. This choice is based on how well it addresses the emotional and social friction points found in our research while remaining highly intuitive for the user.
Comparison Against Requirements 
Design 2 provides a strong foundation by supporting Design Requirement 2 (Visual Outfit Preview) through the collage feature and Design Requirement 4 (Saved Outfit Archive) via the timestamped library. While Design 1 (Virtual Avatar) offered a high-tech solution for the “Spatial Cognition Gap,” its complexity and lack of historical tracking made it a less versatile starting point.
Acknowledging Tradeoffs 
We recognize that Design 2 relies on manual user discipline more than the automated filtering of Design 3 (Context-Aware Inventory Manager). However, our research shows that users value personal expression and “creative spark” over purely logistical dressing. While a manual archive could be empty on first use, it matches existing mental models of some users who already utilize “Instagram archives” to remember their looks. We believe the trade-off of manual upkeep is justified because it removes the mental burden of memorizing past combinations. Instead of relying on memory to ensure variety and social appropriateness, the user can rely on a verified record, significantly reducing morning cognitive load.
Reasoning for Selection 
Design 2 hits the most significant pain points of the Trial Phase such the frustration of physically coordinating pieces or remembering if you had worn an outfit in front of some crowd before and the Finalizing Phase (the time-pressed panic that results in boring yet safe outfits). By providing a solution for the morning bottleneck through a verified library of past successes, we can significantly reduce the decision fatigue and physical effort identified in our experience map.
Future Development 
Design 2 serves as the best foundation because it captures the creative and social aspects of dressing. We intend to use this as a core that can eventually integrate logistical elements from Design 3, such as automated weather and activity tracking, as the system evolves to become even more context-aware.

Paper Prototype Explanation
Building on our selected design direction (Design 2: Visual Collage & Timestamped Archive), we developed a low-fidelity paper prototype to simulate the full interaction flow of assembling, saving, retrieving, and logging outfits. The prototype operationalizes the tasks defined in our storyboards and allows users to move through the system as they would in a mobile application. A short demonstration video (linked here: Paper Prototype Demonstration) illustrates the states and transitions described below.
Navigation and Archive Access
The prototype begins at a home page containing a “My Closet” button. Selecting this button transitions the user to the catalogue page, which displays previously saved outfits as complete visual collages. This implements the archive-centered structure shown in the high-level storyboards, where users can quickly retrieve verified outfits during time-pressured scenarios.
Creating a New Outfit (Visual Collage – Storyboard 3)
From the catalogue page, users can click the “New Outfit” button to enter the closet view. The closet page displays all clothing items in the user’s inventory. Items can be filtered based on weather, colour, and other attributes, reflecting the constraint-based filtering described in the screen-level storyboard.
Users can then drag and drop clothing items onto a designated collage area to assemble an outfit visually. Once satisfied, they click the “Save” button. Upon saving, the newly assembled outfit appears in the catalogue as a complete look. This interaction directly supports the Visual Outfit Preview requirement by allowing users to coordinate pieces digitally before physically dressing.
Viewing and Verifying an Outfit (Storyboard 4)
From the catalogue page, users can click on any saved outfit to enter a large-view mode. In this view, the outfit is displayed prominently, and users can review contextual history information, including when it was previously worn, the event, and the associated audience. This supports efficient retrieval and verification during the morning bottleneck scenario.
Logging Social Context (Storyboards 5 & 6)
In large-view mode, users can click the “I wore this” button to log a new wear instance. This transitions to a logging page where users can:
Modify the date
Select or create an event
Select or create an audience
When setting an event, users can assign a default audience (e.g., a specific class and classmates), streamlining repeated logging for recurring contexts. After saving, the outfit’s history updates to include the new entry, reinforcing the system’s function as a social verification tool.
Adding New Clothing Items
Within the closet page, users can click a “+” button to add new clothing items. The system prompts them to take a photo or select an existing image. The prototype simulates automatic detection of clothing pieces within the image, allowing users to confirm and add those pieces to their digital closet. Once added, returning to the closet view shows the new items available for outfit creation.
Together, these flows demonstrate the full lifecycle of outfit planning, saving, retrieving, and logging within the system.
Key Design Decisions and Rationale
Archive-Centered Structure
The catalogue of saved outfits functions as the system’s anchor. Rather than prioritizing raw inventory, the design centers on complete, verified outfits. This reinforces the goal of reducing decision fatigue by allowing users to rely on previously successful combinations.
Drag-and-Drop Visual Collage
The drag-and-drop interaction was chosen to mirror the physical act of laying out clothes and visually assembling a look. This was intended to bridge the gap between mental visualization and physical trial, aligning with the Visual Outfit Preview requirement.
Integrated Social Logging
Logging wear history directly from the outfit’s large-view page ensures that social tracking remains tied to specific outfit combinations rather than individual items. This preserves the contextual integrity of the Social History Log requirement.
Event-Based Audience Defaults
Allowing events to carry default audiences reflects real-world patterns where certain social contexts involve consistent groups of people. This design choice reduces repetitive data entry while preserving user control.
Advantages of the Current Prototype
End-to-End Task Coverage
The prototype successfully simulates the complete interaction cycle—from creating an outfit to logging its wear—allowing meaningful early evaluation of the chosen design direction.
Clear Social Verification Mechanism
The visible history log in large-view mode makes repetition tracking explicit, directly addressing user anxiety around social outfit reuse.
Structured yet Flexible Filtering
Filtering within the closet page allows users to narrow options based on constraints without eliminating creative freedom during collage assembly.
Limitations of the Current Prototype
Simplified Representation of Automated Detection
The clothing detection feature is simulated in the paper prototype and does not reflect the technical complexity of real-world image recognition, limiting realism during evaluation.
Limited Item-Level Interaction
The prototype does not include a dedicated view mode for individual clothing items. Users can only interact with items within the context of full outfits, which limits exploration and item-specific editing.


No Outfit Editing After Saving
 Once an outfit is saved to the catalogue, there is no mechanism to modify or swap individual components. This restricts flexibility and may require users to recreate an outfit from scratch if small adjustments are needed.


Restricted Outfit Structure
 The collage system assumes one top, one bottom, and one pair of shoes. There is no support for layering or adding accessories, which limits the representation of more complex outfits.


Limited Metadata and Manual Input Options
 The prototype does not allow users to manually add or edit detailed information about clothing items (e.g., material, weather suitability). Additionally, if automatic photo-based detection fails to recognize an item, there is no fallback mechanism for manual entry.









Early evaluation methods and findings
For the evaluation methods we chose to do heuristic evaluations with our classmates acting as experts, and think-aloud evaluations with target users. For the heuristic evaluation, we used Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics, collecting a log of usability problems categorized by Heuristic Number and Severity Rank (0–4) and any qualitative notes from the experts on issues or places to improve (See Appendices A, B and C). We had 5 classmates acting as expert evaluators. Since they are familiar with UX standards and Nielsen’s Heuristics, they could objectively catch system flaws—like missing "emergency exits" or inconsistent labels—that we might have missed because we are too close to the design. For the think-aloud evaluations, we looked for 4–6 university students (ages 18–25) who regularly deal with outfit creation in the morning (See Appendices D and E). We needed to see if our collage and archive logic actually felt intuitive to them and if it successfully offloads the mental burden of remembering past outfits. We found participants through our questionnaire from G1, and by scouting students in study spaces who seemed interested in fashion, and conducted 4 interviews. Our note takers collected verbal “stream-of-consciousness” feedback, task completion rates, and events where users got stuck or confused. This data shows us where the user's mental model doesn't match our design and whether the app actually reduces their cognitive load.
These evaluations were designed to test whether the implemented prototype meaningfully supported our core design requirements—particularly Visual Outfit Preview (Requirement 2), Saved Outfit Archive (Requirement 4), and Social History Log (Requirement 5)—and whether the interaction flows defined in our storyboards reduced cognitive and social friction as intended.
Finding 1: The “drag and drop” interaction when building outfits in their closet
Observation 
Across multiple evaluation sessions, participants consistently struggled with the “drag and drop” functionality used to build outfits in the closet. During the heuristic evaluation (Round 3), evaluators noted confusion about the drag and drop option (violating H9: Help Recognize/Recover from Errors and H10: Help and Documentation). This was echoed in the think-aloud sessions (Rounds 1, 2, and 4), where users paused, became confused, or tried to click placeholders and preexisting clothing items instead of dragging and dropping them.
Interpretation 
A core design goal of this app is to reduce the friction people face when trying on multiple outfits. The “drag and drop” mechanism was designed to emulate real-life planning (Nielsen's H2: Match between system and real world) where people take their clothes and arrange them in their room, but it actually introduced more friction because it clashed with users’ standard mental models for mobile interfaces. Users intuitively expected a tap-to-select or tap-to-swap interaction. This friction directly affects Requirement 2 (Visual Outfit Preview), as difficulty assembling outfits undermines the system’s ability to reduce physical and cognitive effort during outfit planning.
Why it matters / Design Implication 
Because outfit creation is the primary task of the app, any friction here is a major barrier. In the next iteration, the design should pivot away from drag-and-drop toward a more standard mobile interaction pattern, such as automatically placing the clothes in the outfit box on click.


Finding 2: Clarity of system design (friction in basic navigation)
Observation 
Users experienced significant friction when trying to figure out how to log an outfit. In the heuristic evaluation (Round 3), an expert noted that in view mode, the “I wore this” button looked like an unclickable tag rather than an actionable button (violating H4: Consistency and Standards). In both the heuristic evaluation and the think-aloud sessions, users paused at the “history”, unsure if it and its contents were clickable. Additionally, they appeared confused by the “history,” as its purpose was not immediately apparent and they had to explore its functionality to understand what it did. In the heuristic evaluation (Round 2, Round 5), experts noted it would be useful to have more information on existing data, such as the amount of clothing or the amount of outfits (Violating H8: Aesthetic and Minimalist Design and H1: Visibility of System Status).
Interpretation
A secondary design goal of the app is to successfully “offload the mental burden of remembering past outfits”. To achieve this, logging a daily outfit and viewing past logs needs to be instantaneous and obvious. When calls-to-action like "I wore this" blend in as tags, or the “history” requires exploration just to understand its purpose, users experience unnecessary cognitive load trying to navigate the app. This friction in basic navigation directly undermines the app's core purpose of making outfit tracking effortless. Having information like easy to find outfit counts, further reduces mental load and saves users time.  Because social logging supports Requirement 5 (Social History Log), any ambiguity in this flow weakens the system’s ability to reliably function as a social verification tool.
Why it matters / Design Implication 
The findings suggest that the chosen visual cues, terminology, and workflows are failing to guide the user intuitively. For the next iteration, the UI needs clearer signifiers—specifically ensuring that actionable items like buttons look distinctly clickable and do not resemble static tags. Terminology must also be refined to be more representative of the intended action so users do not have to guess the purpose of features like the “history”. 


Finding 3: Flexibility in use 
Observation
Evaluators noted several moments in which they may have wanted to execute a process differently, or view information in a different way. In the heuristic evaluation (Round 4) an evaluator noted that the “Daily Recap” took a lot of steps and time to input information (Violating H2: Match Between System and Real World and H7: Flexibility and Efficiency of Use). In Round 2, the evaluator noted a desire for an “add all” button so they could more quickly add clothing to their closet (Violating H7: Flexibility and Efficiency of Use). In Round 5, the evaluator noted that events may pile up and lead to inefficiency of scrolling and searching for events (Violating H7: Flexibility and Efficiency of Use). In the think-aloud sessions, users noted desire for different ways of viewing several types of information, for example seeing the closet sorted by date (Round 1), wanting to be able to layer or add accessories (Round 1, Round 2), changing a part of a preexisting outfit (Round 2), to have preselects in their closet (Round 4), have edit and view next to each other (Round 4).
Interpretation
There are different ways in which users may want to view information and use the app that depend on their schedule, stress levels, time, style, and the type of person that they are. Being able to view information and execute processes in a more personally intuitive way contributes to our goal of reducing mental load in choosing outfits, as they can do it in a way that more accurately mimics how they might do it in person (but without taking up the same amount of time). It also helps them remember who they wore an outfit in front of (one of our design directions) better if they can view it in a way that makes more sense for them (calendar, recency, date, etc.).  These limitations also affect Requirement 4 (Saved Outfit Archive), as inflexible viewing and logging structures may discourage consistent long-term use of the archive.
Why it matters / Design Implication: 
The findings suggest that the design is failing to most efficiently and effectively help users accomplish their goals on the app. In future implementations, there should be more flexibility in use, where users can specify how they would like to view information, and processes should have simplification options for users who don’t want to log as much information. 







Design implications
Chosen Design Direction
Our design direction focuses on reducing the friction people encounter when trying on multiple outfits in the morning and reducing the mental burden of remembering outfits and if they’ve worn it in front of specific people. While the early evaluation methods suggest that these goals resonate with users, our current design has not been as effective as we had hoped in achieving that goal. Instead of streamlining the experience, elements of our design such as unclear buttons and user flows that require more steps than necessary are increasing cognitive effort and making tasks feel more complicated than they should be. Moving forward, we will be shifting toward a more automated, yet flexible approach that aligns with familiar mobile-standard interactions. The default experience should simplify and automate the workflow, minimizing manual effort for common tasks. At the same time, users with more specific needs should be able to adjust or override these defaults, ensuring flexibility without increasing cognitive load for everyone. These implications are derived directly from the usability breakdowns observed during evaluation and reflect where the current prototype diverged from our intended design goals.


Changes to be made in the next iteration
The following changes address specific friction points identified across both heuristic and think-aloud evaluations. 
Based on our evaluation findings, here are the key changes we plan to implement in the next iteration of our design:
Replace drag-and-drop with click-to-select interactions: Drag-and-drop caused unnecessary confusion when building an outfit. Instead, clicking an item should automatically place it into the outfit box, following familiar mobile interaction patterns.
Clarify buttons and terminology: Some actions were not clearly distinguishable from unclickable labels. For example, “I wore this” should feel like an obvious action, and “history” should better reflect what the feature actually does for easier navigation of the system.
Make system status more visible: Displaying data such as clothing counts or total outfits provides users with immediate, useful information about the current state of their digital closet.
Improve event management in the scroll view: Users should be able to delete events or reorder them (by recency, popularity, or day) so the feed doesn’t become overwhelming or cluttered.
Introduce a simplified Daily Recap option: Not every user wants to navigate multiple steps just to log an outfit. A streamlined, optional fast-track version reduces friction, while keeping the full manual flow available for users who prefer more control.
Add an “add all” option for clothing uploads: When multiple items are detected in one photo, users should be able to add them all at once instead of confirming each individually.
Enable filtering and flexible viewing options: Instead of forcing users to scroll through a chronological feed, they should be able to filter by date, audience, and event and switch between views like calendar or recency-based sorting.
Support layering and accessories: Users expressed interest in building more complex outfits, such as adding multiple tops or accessories, so the system should support that flexibility.
Add smart preselects and automatic filters: Instead of requiring users to manually narrow down their closet every time, the system should automatically apply filters based on context (i.e. weather conditions) or generate a randomized preselect of suitable items to reduce mental effort.
Place “edit” and “view” side-by-side: These actions are often used together, so positioning them next to each other makes the flow more intuitive.


Reflection on Limitations and Uncertainty
While these evaluations provided actionable insights, several limitations in our methodology introduce uncertainty into our findings and affect our overall confidence in the current design direction.
Lack of Consensus and Incomplete Data in Heuristic Evaluations
There was a notable lack of consensus among our expert evaluators, with “drag and drop” being the only issue consistently identified across multiple sheets. Furthermore, experts frequently didn’t add enough detail to the heuristic evaluation forms as they tested, leading to gaps in our formal data. This inconsistency means some usability issues may have been missed or overemphasized based on the specific evaluator's focus.
Potential Observer Bias
Much of our observational data was recorded from the perspective of team members who have worked extensively on the project. This familiarity may have led to a biased interpretation of user pauses or errors, potentially overlooking fundamental flaws that a neutral observer would have caught.
Small Sample Size and Low Participant Engagement
Our think-aloud sessions were limited by a small number of participants who provided relatively few verbal comments. This lack of “stream-of-consciousness” data makes it difficult to definitively map out the user's mental model or understand exactly why certain friction points occurred beyond what we could observe visually.

Appendix B: Heuristic Evaluation Instruments
Nielsen’s Heuristics:

H1	Visibility of System Status 
Keep users informed about the system's current state with timely and appropriate feedback.
H2	Match Between System and Real World 
Use familiar language and real-world conventions rather than technical jargon.
H3	User Control and Freedom 
Provide exits to undo actions. Support autonomy through customization and opt-out choices.
H4	Consistency and Standards 
Follow industry and platform conventions so users aren’t confused by varying terms/actions.
H5	Error Prevention 
Prevent slips/mistakes before they happen, or ask for confirmation for high-stakes actions.
H6	Recognition Rather Than Recall 
Make information/actions visible so users don’t have to remember details from one screen to the next.
H7	Flexibility and Efficiency of Use 
Provide shortcuts for frequent actions to help expert users work more quickly.
H8	Aesthetic and Minimalist Design 
Keep the interface clean and focused by removing irrelevant or rarely used information.
H9	Help Recognize/Recover from Errors 
Use plain language for error messages. Clearly state the problem. Suggest a direct solution.
H10	Help and Documentation 
Ensure necessary help content is easy to search and focused on the user’s specific tasks.

Script:
Hi! Thanks so much for taking the time to join us today. We’re working on building an app that helps people get dressed. We’re concentrating on reducing the friction people face when they have to try on multiple outfits in the morning and remembering past outfits and who they wore them in front of. What you can see is a paper prototype of the app which we’d like you to experiment with. Once we start, I’ll stop talking and we ask that you talk aloud about everything, what you’re doing or trying to do, what you’re thinking, why, and anything else that pops into your head in this process. For example “I’m going to click on this” and you can click the paper or “I’m not sure what to do here”. There is no offending us with what you do, we want to see how a user would actually interact with this and see how we can improve. I’ll be your facilitator, Vennise will be taking notes and BLANK and Arush will be triggering the actions based on what you do in the prototype. We want you to try to complete 2 tasks, one is to make a new outfit and then save it, and the other is to log that you wore an outfit. Let me know if you have any questions and if not we can get started. 


Who participates and why:
4–6 university students (ages 18–25) who regularly deal with outfit creation stress in the morning. We need to see if our collage and archive logic actually feels intuitive to them and if it successfully offloads the mental burden of remembering past outfits.


Type of Data Collected:
Verbal “stream-of-consciousness” feedback, task completion rates, and events where users get stuck or confused. This data shows us where the user's mental model doesn't match our design and whether the app actually reduces their cognitive load.
